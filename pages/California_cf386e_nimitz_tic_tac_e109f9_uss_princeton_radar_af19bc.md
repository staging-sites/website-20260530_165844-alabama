## Introduction

The radar tracking performed by **USS Princeton** during the November 2004 “Nimitz Tic Tac” encounter is central to how this episode became one of the most discussed unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) cases tied to California’s naval history. Trained U.S. Navy radar operators reported a series of **anomalous contacts over several days**, and on the day of the intercept, Princeton’s radar data helped vector fighter jets toward the object later dubbed the “Tic Tac”. Yet the **actual radar recordings, raw data and official analytical reports remain unreleased or incomplete in the public record** — a gap that shapes much of the debate about what the sensors actually revealed and how strongly they support extraordinary performance claims. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://aliencouncil.com/cases/00041-tic-tac-uss-nimitz-2004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aliencouncil.com">[The Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aliencouncil.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">00041 tic tac uss nimitz 2004</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The CouncilCase #00041 — USS Nimitz — &quot;Tic Tac&quot; encounter, 14 November 2004 · The CouncilMay 8, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2026</span></span></span>

## Radar System Capabilities and Limitations

The Princeton’s primary sensor was the **AN/SPY‑1B** radar, part of the Aegis Combat System — a **multi‑function 3D, phased array radar** used by U.S. Navy cruisers for air and surface surveillance, automatic detection and tracking, and weapon guidance. AN/SPY‑1 variants can track hundreds of targets simultaneously and provide **continuous 360° coverage** without mechanical movement, a capability that made the Princeton well‑suited to monitor multiple radar returns over extended engagements. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.missiledefenseadvocacy.org/defense-systems/anspy-1-radar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: missiledefenseadvocacy.org">[Missile Defense Advocacy Alliance]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">missiledefenseadvocacy.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missile Defense Advocacy AllianceAN/SPY-1 RadarThe SPY-1 can maintain continuous radar surveillance while automatically tracking more tha...</span></span></span>

However, while powerful, the SPY‑1 is *not* a precision scientific instrument designed for reconstructing fine motion profiles of unknown small objects. Its strengths lie in **air defence threat tracking**, not in high‑resolution flight‑path reconstruction for unidentified targets, and clutter rejection over the sea can be challenging — especially for small, low‑altitude returns that might resemble environmental or non‑aerodynamic targets. These technical traits mean that **radar signatures alone cannot definitively characterise the physical nature or propulsion of a target** without corroborating sensor data. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.missiledefenseadvocacy.org/defense-systems/anspy-1-radar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: missiledefenseadvocacy.org">[Missile Defense Advocacy Alliance]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">missiledefenseadvocacy.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missile Defense Advocacy AllianceAN/SPY-1 RadarThe SPY-1 can maintain continuous radar surveillance while automatically tracking more tha...</span></span></span>

## Reported Object Tracks During the Nimitz Exercise

According to testimony from Princeton crew members and later accounts reconstructed by researchers and media outlets, radar operators aboard the Princeton tracked **multiple anomalous contacts for nearly two weeks before the main intercept**. These returns were described as showing objects appearing at **very high altitudes (~80 000 ft)** and, at times, descending to near sea level in short spans of time — behaviour alleged by some operators to be inconsistent with known aerodynamic flight envelopes. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://aliencouncil.com/cases/00041-tic-tac-uss-nimitz-2004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aliencouncil.com">[The Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aliencouncil.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">00041 tic tac uss nimitz 2004</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The CouncilCase #00041 — USS Nimitz — &quot;Tic Tac&quot; encounter, 14 November 2004 · The CouncilMay 8, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2026</span></span></span>

On **14 November 2004**, the radar operators reportedly saw a track that prompted them to vector F/A‑18F Super Hornets from the **USS Nimitz** toward the contact. After the visual and infrared encounter, some operators — according to post‑event summaries — claimed that radar reacquired the object about **60 miles away at the planned combat air patrol point within less than a minute**, implying speeds beyond known aircraft capabilities. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://searchufos.com/cases/uss-nimitz-tic-tac.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: searchufos.com">[Search UFOs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">searchufos.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Search UFOs USS Nimitz Tic Tac Encounter — UFO Case File</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Search UFOsUSS Nimitz Tic Tac Encounter — UFO Case FileNovember 14, 2004...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2004</span></span></span>

## Analysis of Gaps in Publicly Released Radar Data

Despite the operational management of these radar tracks during the event, **the complete Princeton radar tapes and raw SPY‑1 data for November 2004 have not been declassified or released publicly**. Independent researchers and sceptical analysts note that the classification or absence of these high‑fidelity [records]({{ 'records/' | relative_url }}) means that **the most dramatic performance claims rely on operator testimony rather than transparent datasets** that scientists could independently verify. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://aliencouncil.com/cases/00041-tic-tac-uss-nimitz-2004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aliencouncil.com">[The Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aliencouncil.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">00041 tic tac uss nimitz 2004</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The CouncilCase #00041 — USS Nimitz — &quot;Tic Tac&quot; encounter, 14 November 2004 · The CouncilMay 8, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2026</span></span></span>

This evidentiary gap clouds key inferences: without access to the actual track files, it is impossible for independent analysts to confirm the altitude, velocity, acceleration, or continuity of the purported radar contacts, or to rule out artifacts, clutter responses or misinterpretations within the SPY‑1 processing chain. In radar research, small changes in settings, signal processing thresholds, or environmental factors can produce **false or ambiguous tracks**, especially near radar sensitivity limits — and this uncertainty underlines why critics stress that the available public record does not provide a **definitive proof of exotic performance**. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.missiledefenseadvocacy.org/defense-systems/anspy-1-radar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: missiledefenseadvocacy.org">[Missile Defense Advocacy Alliance]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">missiledefenseadvocacy.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missile Defense Advocacy AllianceAN/SPY-1 RadarThe SPY-1 can maintain continuous radar surveillance while automatically tracking more tha...</span></span></span>

## Why Radar Evidence Matters and What It Does Not Prove

Radar tracking from a capable system like the AN/SPY‑1B aboard USS Princeton certainly adds weight to the Nimitz “[Tic Tac]({{ 'tic-tac/' | relative_url }})” episode compared with anecdotal sightings lacking sensor corroboration. Multi‑sensor reports that combine trained operators, naval radar systems, airborne early‑warning platforms, and infrared video are rare in UAP records and contribute to the case’s prominence. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://aliencouncil.com/cases/00041-tic-tac-uss-nimitz-2004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aliencouncil.com">[The Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aliencouncil.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">00041 tic tac uss nimitz 2004</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The CouncilCase #00041 — USS Nimitz — &quot;Tic Tac&quot; encounter, 14 November 2004 · The CouncilMay 8, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2026</span></span></span>

However, **without the raw radar data publicly available**, it is not possible to *quantitatively* confirm claims of extraordinary motion or performance beyond known physics. Testimony that an object “appeared” at vastly different locations does not, by itself, constitute a scientifically reconstructable track.
